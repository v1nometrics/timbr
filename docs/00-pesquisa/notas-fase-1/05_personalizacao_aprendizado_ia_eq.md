# Personalizing EQ to listener preference and hearing, continuous preference learning, and natural-language / LLM-driven EQ (state of the art as of Nov 2026)

Scope note: this file covers preference science, commercial personalization products, preference-learning algorithms, semantic/NL-to-EQ, context detection to switch EQ, hearing tests on Mac, on-device vs cloud ML, and community reception. It does not cover EQ DSP implementation, audio routing, device control, or music/lyrics recognition. Dates are given wherever a source is older than 2025. "Peer-reviewed" is marked where known. Many AES convention papers are reviewed only at the abstract/precis level, and arXiv preprints are not peer-reviewed unless a venue is listed.

## 1. Preference science: Harman target research, listener segments, age/hearing, loudness, how far and how stably individuals deviate from a target

### Takeaway
Harman's research (Olive, Welti et al., 2013–2025) shows that a population target, basically "more bass and less treble than diffuse-field", is the best single choice for most listeners with stereo music. Individual preferences cluster into a few segments that differ mainly in **bass shelf level (spread of roughly 7 dB) and treble tilt (roughly 7 dB)**. Membership in those segments is shaped by age, gender, training and possibly hearing loss. Newer analyses caution that the clusters are unstable across clustering methods and that personal settings add little predictive power beyond a headphone's average rating. Evidence that preference changes with music **genre** is weak or null. Evidence that it changes with **content type** (stereo vs binaural/spatial) and that bass preference **co-varies with playback level** is stronger.

### Cited Findings
**Origins of the target (older work, 2013–2018)**
- Olive, Welti & McMullin (2013, AES 135th Conv., Paper 8994) had listeners adjust bass and treble on an HD 800 "replicator" (virtual headphone). They found a "clear broad strokes trend" toward more bass and/or less treble than a strictly flat (diffuse-field) response — [headphones.com summary](https://headphones.com/blogs/features/what-headphone-sound-profiles-do-people-like); [AES e-lib 17042](https://aes.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=17042)
- Olive & Welti (2015, AES Paper 9382, "Factors That Influence Listeners' Preferred Bass and Treble Levels in Headphones"): **249 listeners** adjusted relative bass and treble. Preferred balance depended on **program, age, gender and prior listening experience**. Younger, less-experienced listeners leaned toward more bass, and individual taste and training produced significant variation — [AES e-lib 17940](https://aes.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=17940); [AES forum entry](https://secure.aes.org/forum/pubs/conventions/?elib=17940)
- Olive, Welti & Khonsaripour (2018, AES 144th, Paper 9919) used 31 headphone responses and 130 trained and untrained listeners to build a statistical model that predicts preference ratings of around-ear and on-ear headphones — [headphones.com](https://headphones.com/blogs/features/what-headphone-sound-profiles-do-people-like); [AES e-lib 19436](https://aes.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=19436)
- The Harman target is a population average, not a universal rule. Critics note that sizeable segments prefer more or less bass than it — [QFI Audio explainer](https://www.qfiaudio.com/learn/harman-target)

**Listener segments (2019)**
- Olive, Welti & Khonsaripour (2019, AES 146th, Paper 10156, "Segmentation of Listeners Based on Their Preferred Headphone Sound Quality Profiles") used agglomerative hierarchical clustering and found three listener classes. These are commonly summarized as **64% prefer Harman, 15% prefer more bass, 21% prefer less bass** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332210946_Segmentation_of_Listeners_Based_on_Their_Preferred_Headphone_Sound_Quality_Profiles); [AES e-lib 20289](https://aes.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=20289)
- The "Less Bass Is Better" class is disproportionately female (40% of females vs 13% of males) and over 50 (50%). The authors flag **hearing loss as a possible confound**. The "more bass" class is small, with few female or older members, so boosting bass well beyond target risks alienating most listeners — [Acoustics Today, Spring 2022 (Olive), p.61–63](https://acousticstoday.org/issues/2022AT/Spring2022/files/basic-html/page63.html)
- The 55+ group preferred a brighter headphone with less bass. A possible explanation is age-related hearing loss, where extra treble and less bass aid intelligibility. The authors say more research is needed — [Acoustics Today, Spring 2022](https://acousticstoday.org/issues/2022AT/Spring2022/files/basic-html/page63.html)
- Olive in interview: the segmentation suggests "there are good reasons to slightly deviate [from] the target to satisfy different tastes based on age, hearing or listening experiences" — [PS Audio Copper #189](https://www.psaudio.com/blogs/copper/engaging-and-enthralling-music-for-all-talking-with-dr-sean-olive-of-harman-international-part-one)
- **Critique of the segment numbers.** A 2025–2026 headphones.com analysis says the 64/15/21 shorthand is "not a totally accurate description". The Harman target was the #1 response even within the "more bass" class. Many commercial headphones were rated statistically close to it in Class 1 (Harman 70, Sony MDR-7506 67, Shure SRH-840 66, Oppo PM-1 66, Philips Fidelio X1 65, HD 800S 63, DT 990 63, un-rescaled). The 2019 linear anchor-stretching rescaling exaggerated differences among top performers. Very different curves (e.g., the flat-bass Oppo PM-1) can be equally preferred — [headphones.com](https://headphones.com/blogs/features/what-headphone-sound-profiles-do-people-like)

**2025–2026 updates (in-ear MOA, cluster instability)**
- Olive (Oct 2025, AES), "The Preferred In-Ear Headphone Target Curve Based on Method of Adjustments": **36 mostly trained listeners** adjusted bass, a 3 kHz band and treble. Cluster analysis found **4 segments whose preferences span +2 to +9 dB bass boost and −5 to +2 dB treble relative to the diffuse-field response** — [AES France section event page](https://france.aessections.org/event/reglages-preferentiels-des-ecouteurs-intra-auriculaires-par-sean-olive/); [AES e-lib 22928](https://aes.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=22928) (abstract not directly retrievable, 403)
- In a 2025 method-of-adjustment IEM study, eight listeners preferred a roughly 0 dB bass-to-treble delta. The headphones.com author excluded them as unrepresentative and says the 2025 results "controvert most of the established literature" on IEM bass — [headphones.com](https://headphones.com/blogs/features/what-headphone-sound-profiles-do-people-like)
- Olive & Clark (2025, AES Express Paper 394) derived a preferred target for the newer B&K 5128 and GRAS 45CA-10 fixtures — [headphones.com](https://headphones.com/blogs/features/what-headphone-sound-profiles-do-people-like); [AES e-lib 23068](https://aes.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=23068)
- A Sept 2026 Headphonesty article on Olive's data reports four listener groups. It says **membership shifted when the clustering method or data scaling changed**, quotes Olive that the clusters should not be treated as fixed or universal listener types, and says **personal settings added little predictive power beyond each headphone's average rating**. I saw this only via search snippets (the page returned 403). It describes "249 listeners", which may conflate the 2015 and 2025 studies — [Headphonesty, Sept 2026](https://www.headphonesty.com/2026/09/listeners-agree-headphones-sound-sean-olive-data/)

**Content and genre dependence**
- Engel, Alon, Scheumann & Mehra (Facebook Reality Labs, AES AVAR 2020). Of 21 listeners, 16 passed a consistency post-screen; each did 80 ratings including 2 repetitions, with 4 audio materials and 2 content types. The **Harman target was rated significantly higher for stereo music**, but an **individually calibrated flat response was preferred for binaural/spatial content**. Authors: "content-dependent HpEQ would be beneficial". Note that 5 of 21 listeners were excluded for inconsistent repeated ratings — [Engel et al. 2020 PDF](https://research.facebook.com/file/283503763627038/Listener-Preferred-Headphone-Frequency-Response-for-Stereo-and-Spatial-Audio-Content.pdf)
- Lorho (2009, AES), 80 listeners: a target with a lower-amplitude 3 kHz peak than diffuse-field was preferred **for both music and speech**. This suggests some content-independence (snippet-level) — [AES e-lib 14966](https://www.aes.org/e-lib/browse.cfm?elib=14966)
- Interactive Differential Evolution study (FORCE SenseLab / Univ. of Aizu, Dec 2025 preprint): **no significant track effect** on preference across 8 tracks (χ²(7) = 9.974, p = .190) — [arXiv 2512.08313](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08313v1)
- An MDPI paper (2022) argues EQ is context-dependent: "the same equalization curve can have different effect on different songs", and preference differs by age, gender and experience — [MDPI Acoustics 4(3):45](https://mdpi.com/2624-599X/4/3/45)
- RTINGS ("One Curve Doesn't Fit All: 350 Headphone Listening Tests Show That Many Sound Profiles Can Be Preferred"). Per search snippet: 35 employee listeners, five curves and two songs, with no single clearly preferred curve. The full text is paywalled and was not verified — [RTINGS](https://www.rtings.com/headphones/learn/research/frequency-response-1-8)
- I found no controlled study that measured the same listeners' preferred headphone EQ across music genres and found a large genre effect. A Bose patent proposes genre presets (more bass for rap, more treble for classical), but that is a design idea, not evidence — [US patent 8942385](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8942385)

**Loudness / level**
- An AES paper on bass and loudness preference (loudspeakers vs headphones) found that **listeners who preferred more bass also preferred louder playback**, in both conditions. Most chose slightly higher bass and loudness on loudspeakers than on headphones. Settings were made across several genres, and the paper also examined taste, hearing ability and training (title not verified, from snippet) — [AES e-lib 19252](https://www.aes.org/e-lib/browse.cfm?elib=19252)
- Informal: a SoundGuys reader poll found a ~+5 dB bass boost most popular (~48% of votes). Commenters said the right amount depends on the track. This is anecdotal — [SoundGuys bass poll](https://www.soundguys.com/bass-poll-results-114513)

**Per-unit / per-ear variation relevant to "ideal EQ per headphone"**
- "Headphone behavior varies based on the ear it is placed on", notably for the AKG K712 Pro. Fixtures with KB50xx pinnae and the B&K 5128 overestimate treble above ~4 kHz relative to human ears — [headphones.com](https://headphones.com/blogs/features/what-headphone-sound-profiles-do-people-like)

### Inferences
- The scientifically defensible personalization space is **low-dimensional**: a measured-headphone correction to a population target, plus a few personal offsets (low-shelf gain ~105 Hz, high-shelf/tilt, maybe a 3 kHz presence band). The published segment spreads suggest offsets of about +2…+9 dB bass and −5…+2 dB treble relative to DF. Free-form 10-band "personal curves" have no scientific backing as a starting point.
- Because cluster membership is unstable and personal settings add little beyond headphone quality (per the 2026 summary), the app should present personalization as a **small, reversible offset around a good target**, and keep a one-click "reference/target only" state.
- Genre-specific EQ is not supported by controlled evidence. Content-type-specific EQ (stereo vs binaural/Atmos-rendered spatial) is supported. Level-coupled bass (more bass at lower volume) is plausible and consistent with equal-loudness behaviour, but per-listener curves are not established. Logging playback level with every preference judgement would be valuable.
- Age and hearing are plausible **priors**, e.g. initializing older users toward the "less bass/brighter" region. They should not be hard rules, because the source itself flags confounding.
- High intra-listener inconsistency (5/21 excluded in Engel et al.) means any learning system must model **noise in user judgements** and use repeated/consistency trials.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the full 2015, 2019 or 2025 AES papers (paywall/403), so effect sizes for age and gender, test-retest reliability and per-program results are from abstracts and secondary sources. The exact sample size of the 2025 study (36 per AES France vs "249" per Headphonesty) conflicts.
- I found no peer-reviewed study that measured **long-term stability** (weeks/months) of an individual's preferred EQ, or the effect of adaptation/familiarity.
- No direct study was found on how preferred bass boost changes **within a listener** as playback level changes. ISO 226 equal-loudness data were not retrieved in this pass.

## 2. Commercial personalization products and their test protocols

### Takeaway
Commercial products use three protocol families. (a) **Threshold tests**, i.e. simplified pure-tone audiometry, Békésy tracking or masked thresholds: Apple Hearing Test, Mimi, Samsung Adapt Sound, Jabra MySound, Audiodo. (b) **Pairwise A/B preference tests**: Sonarworks SoundID, Apple Headphone Accommodations "Custom Audio Setup", Widex SoundSense Learn (Bayesian optimization). (c) **Physiological measurement**: Nura otoacoustic emissions, and Apple Personalized Spatial Audio ear/head scans. Only Apple's Hearing Test and Hearing Aid feature have regulator-reviewed clinical validation. Almost all preference-benefit claims (80–89% "preferred") are vendor-run studies.

### Cited Findings
**Sonarworks SoundID (Listen / mobile app)**
- The protocol is a hearing test first, then an A/B "sound comparison test" as the "second optimization layer". It asks "the least amount of questions in a series of A/B samples to find the sound that's the best suited for you" — [Sonarworks support: A/B test](https://support.sonarworks.com/hc/en-us/articles/20425227710738); [nextpit review](https://nextpit.com/soundworks-soundid-review)
- White paper: the user selects content, is given "2 different sound targets as instantly switchable A and B options", and picks one or indicates no clear preference — [Sonarworks white paper](https://www.sonarworks.com/blog/research/white-paper)
- Reviewers describe it as like an eye test (which of two slightly different EQ versions do you prefer). Engadget noted the effect was more noticeable in clarity/presence than in overall tonal balance — [MusicTech review](https://www.musictech.com/reviews/studio-recording-gear/sonarworks-sound-id-and-sound-id-listen/); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/sonarworks-soundid-listen-personalized-audio-app-mac-pc-130040080.html)
- SoundID Listen (2020–2021) applied the profile system-wide on Mac/PC with cloud-synced profiles and a subscription model. Coverage is mostly 2020–2021 (older) — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/sonarworks-soundid-listen-personalized-audio-app-mac-pc-130040080.html); [What Hi-Fi](https://www.whathifi.com/news/soundid-listen-app-promises-truly-personalised-headphones-listening)
- Vendor claim: >80% of blind-test participants preferred the personalized sound. This is self-reported, not independent — [Sonarworks via search summaries](https://www.sonarworks.com/blog/research/white-paper)
- The predecessor True-Fi is discontinued. I could not confirm SoundID Listen's current (2026) status — [Sonarworks True-Fi page](https://sonarworks.com/truefi)

**Mimi (licensed SDK, used by Beyerdynamic, Skullcandy, Cleer and others)**
- There are two tests. The **masked-threshold (MT) test** measures how quiet a tone can be and still be heard over background noise, which Mimi relates to spectral tuning (broad tuning indicates impairment). The **pure-tone threshold (PTT) test** uses an automated procedure modelled on Békésy tracking — [Mimi MT blog](https://mimi.io/blog/how-to-interpret-your-hearing-test-results-mt-test-for-sound-personalization); [Mimi PTT blog](https://mimi.io/blog/how-to-interpret-your-hearing-test-results-ptt-test-for-sound-personalization)
- Processing is described as dynamic multiband compression plus "loudness loss fitting", not just EQ. The user can choose Richer/Softer intensity — [Mimi technology](https://mimi.io/technology); [Mimi FAQ](https://mimi.io/faq/products-and-solutions)
- The Mimi Hearing Test is **no longer a CE-certified class 1 medical product**, and it now follows the consumer hearing-wellness standard **ANSI/CTA-2118** — [Mimi FAQ](https://mimi.io/faq/products-and-solutions)
- Vendor white paper (Mar 2024): "Mimi Personalized Sound is Highly Preferred by Listeners" — a blind study run by the company, with a pure-tone test on iOS with Apple headphones — [Mimi white paper PDF](https://info.mimi.io/hubfs/Mimi.io%20-%20White%20Papers/Mimi%20Hearing%20Technologies_Mimi%20Personalized%20Sound%20is%20Highly%20Preferred%20by%20Listeners_March%202024.pdf)
- An independent hands-on (TechCrunch) had mixed results: profile saving failed, and profiles did not transfer between devices — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=2232366)

**Apple**
- **Hearing Test (AirPods Pro 2/3, iOS 18.1+)** is based on pure-tone audiometry. It estimates the audiogram with **Gaussian-process classification**, a probabilistic model giving continuous thresholds across frequency. It was clinically validated against reference audiometry to support FDA authorization — [Apple Hearing Health white paper, Oct 2024](https://images.apple.com/health/pdf/Hearing_Health_Features_on_AirPods_Pro_2_October_2024.pdf); [Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2024/10/how-apple-developed-the-worlds-first-end-to-end-hearing-health-experience)
- FDA De Novo DEN230081 (Hearing Aid Feature): a supplemental Hearing Test validation (n = 202) found audiograms and resulting gain profiles consistent with professional audiograms, with **±5 dB for >98% of gain differences**. Use is limited to adults 18+ with perceived mild-to-moderate loss — [FDA De Novo mirror](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/DEN230081)
- **Headphone Accommodations → Custom Audio Setup** (iOS and macOS): the user "listen[s] to audio samples and choose[s] which ones sound better", and the system suggests settings. The user can toggle Custom vs Standard, or import an audiogram from Health (iPhone) — [Mac User Guide](https://support.apple.com/en-my/guide/mac-help/use-headphone-accommodations-mchl8028e24e/mac); [Apple support HT211218](https://support.apple.com/HT211218)
- **Personalized Spatial Audio** uses the iPhone TrueDepth camera to build a representation of ear and head shape. It is processed on-device, images are not stored, and the profile is E2E-synced and used to change rendering. Apple does not explicitly say "HRTF"; that link is press inference — [Apple support 102596](https://support.apple.com/102596); [Apple privacy page](https://www.apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/personalized-spatial-audio/); [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/news/apple-is-adding-personalized-audio-to-airpods-by-scanning-your-ears)

**Samsung Adapt Sound**: choose an age preset or take a test of alternating left/right beeps with yes/no answers in a quiet place. It creates a per-ear profile applied system-wide, framed as an accessibility tool rather than a clinical test — [Samsung support](https://www.samsung.com/latin_en/support/mobile-devices/how-to-customize-the-adapt-sound); [SamMobile](https://www.sammobile.com/news/one-ui-tip-get-the-best-audio-quality-adapt-sound/)

**Jabra MySound (Sound+ app)**: a per-ear tap-when-you-hear-a-tone test. Results are not shown, and EQ is adjusted automatically. A Japanese review says it draws on data from more than 80,000 people and adjusts 10 bands (snippet-level). Jabra states it is not a medical diagnosis — [Voicebot 2020](https://voicebot.ai/2020/06/02/jabra-adds-hearing-test-to-earbuds-for-personalized-listening-experience/); [Gizmodo JP](https://www.gizmodo.jp/article/jabra-mysound/); [Jabra hearing test](https://www.jabra.com/en-au/hearing/hearing-test)

**Audiodo Personal Sound (e.g., in Nothing X app)**: a roughly 3-minute real-time hearing test produces a profile stored on the earbuds. Audiodo claims accuracy "on par with clinical audiometry" and ISO 389 compliance (vendor claims, unverified), plus real-time content-aware EQ — [Nothing support](https://support.nothing.tech/hc/en-us/articles/34147095344529-What-is-Personal-Sound); [Nothing: Personal Sound Profile](https://support.nothing.tech/hc/en-us/articles/38937876863249-What-is-a-Personal-Sound-Profile)

**Nura (2017–, company status uncertain)**: plays tones and measures **otoacoustic emissions** with in-ear microphones to build a profile. It may fail if insufficient OAE response is detected. Nura says it is not optimized for people with specific hearing needs or tinnitus. Reviewers were unsure of its accuracy. Wikipedia describes Nura in the past tense — [Hearing Health Matters 2017](https://hearinghealthmatters.org/blog/2017/otoacoustic-emissions-oaes-personalized-listening-nura-headphones/); [Nura help](https://help.nuraphone.com/en/Will-the-Nuraphone-NuraLoop-or-NuraTrue-work-if-I-have-specific-hearing-needs-e4e7); [Engadget 2017](https://www.engadget.com/2017-10-03-nura-nuraphones-review.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nura_(company))

**Widex SoundSense Learn (hearing aids; the most relevant commercial analogue for preference learning)**
- It uses **Bayesian optimization driven by user A/B comparisons** in the moment to create saved programs — [Thieme, Seminars in Hearing](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/pdf/10.1055/s-0041-1735135.pdf)
- Analysis of real-world logs: finding the desired sound typically takes **≤15 comparisons** — [Hearing Review, Jan 2021](https://hearingreview.com/hearing-products/hearing-aids/ai)
- Lab study: 20 hearing-impaired participants across 12 sound scenarios. It relates to the IHAPS procedure, which seeks an optimum in as few comparisons as possible — [RWTH Aachen](https://publications.rwth-aachen.de/record/769543/files/769543.pdf)
- Vendor figures: 84–89% of participants preferred SSL settings (manufacturer-sourced) — [Widex blog](https://www.widex.com/en/blog/global/soundsense-learn/); [Hearing Review 2020](https://hearingreview.com/hearing-loss/patient-care/hearing-fittings/soundsense-learn-when-average-is-not-enough)

### Inferences
- The two protocol families answer different questions. Threshold tests estimate **audibility** and correct it with gain/compression. A/B tests estimate **taste**. A credible audiophile app should keep them separate, and the audiophile-relevant core is A/B taste learning around a target.
- Widex SSL is the strongest real-world proof that **pairwise A/B + Bayesian optimization converges in ~15 judgements** for a low-dimensional gain space. Sonarworks and Apple Custom Audio Setup confirm that A/B is the consumer-accepted UX.
- Mimi's processing goes beyond EQ (multiband compression). Copying "hearing-test → EQ" naively would not reproduce what these products do, and audiophiles may object to compression.

### Gaps
- **Dirac** (and any Dirac consumer personalization) was not researched in this pass.
- No independent (non-vendor) blind evaluation of SoundID, Mimi, Audiodo, Jabra or Samsung personalization was found.
- The internal algorithms of Sonarworks' A/B sequence (how candidates are chosen) and Apple Custom Audio Setup are not publicly documented in the sources found.
- No source details how AirPods Pro 3 (2025) changed hearing features.

## 3. Algorithms for learning EQ preferences from few interactions

### Takeaway
The best-established approach is **preferential Bayesian optimization**: a Gaussian-process utility over a low-dimensional EQ parameter space, fitted from pairwise A/B choices (probit/Bradley-Terry-type likelihood, Laplace approximation), with an acquisition function such as EUBO choosing the next pair. It has peer-reviewed support in hearing-aid personalization (10–20 comparisons; 6x preference over prescription in a small clinical study) and a commercial deployment (Widex). Interactive evolutionary methods work but are slower (~30 min). Contextual bandits for per-genre/per-context EQ have **no published audio-EQ application found**. New in 2025–2026: BO that accepts **natural-language feedback** via an LLM (LILO, ICML 2026).

### Cited Findings
- **Nielsen et al., "Perception-Based Personalization of Hearing Aids Using Gaussian Processes and Active Learning"** (IEEE/ACM TASLP, ~2015): a GP models the listener's internal preference function from pairwise judgements, so users need not memorize ratings. **10–20 pairwise assessments** sufficed for ten hearing-impaired subjects (snippet-level figure) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/278402217_Perception-Based_Personalization_of_Hearing_Aids_Using_Gaussian_Processes_and_Active_Learning); [DTU tech report](http://www2.imm.dtu.dk/pubdb/edoc/imm6848.pdf)
- **Ni, Lobarinas & Kehtarnavaz (arXiv 2406.09634, Jun 2024)**: per-band Bayesian learning run simultaneously across bands, trained on paired comparisons. It approached the true preference function in fewer comparisons than prior ML methods in simulation. In a clinical test with **8 hearing-impaired subjects**, personalized gains were on average **6× more preferred than standard prescriptive gains** — [arXiv 2406.09634](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09634)
- Multi-task/hierarchical GP preference learning (2010) improved prediction of hearing-impaired listeners' sound-quality preferences by sharing information across users. This is a basis for cold-start priors from population data — [Neurocomputing 2010](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231210000251)
- An AES paper uses sequential Bayesian GP regression to model each listener's preferences, demonstrated on a **five-band equalizer** — [AES e-lib 17048](https://www.aes.org/e-lib/browse.cfm?elib=17048)
- "Sequential BO with pairwise comparison" frames hearing-aid/EQ fitting as preference learning, because tuning results "cannot be objectively evaluated" — [arXiv 2103.13192](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.13192)
- 2024 review of ML approaches to hearing-aid amplification personalization, covering pairwise-feedback approaches — [MDPI Sensors 24(5):1546](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/5/1546)
- Pairwise outcomes are binary, so the GP posterior is non-Gaussian. The standard approach is a **probit likelihood + Laplace approximation** — [arXiv 2503.15407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.15407)
- **Open implementation: BoTorch `PairwiseGP`** is a probit-likelihood GP learned from pairwise data via a Laplace approximation, with a scaled RBF kernel by default. BoTorch provides analytic **EUBO** (Lin et al. 2022) and **qEUBO** (Astudillo et al. 2023) acquisition for choosing the next comparison. Caveat: noise σ is implicitly fixed at 1 — [BoTorch preference acquisition source](https://botorch.readthedocs.io/en/stable/_modules/botorch/acquisition/preference.html); [BoTorch PairwiseGP source](https://pytorch.org/botorch/api/_modules/botorch/models/pairwise_gp.html)
- BoFire tutorial "Preference Learning with the Pairwise GP" wraps BoTorch PairwiseGP, uses EUBO to pick the next pair, and notes that only the sign of preference, not its magnitude, is used — [BoFire tutorial](https://experimental-design.github.io/bofire/docs/tutorials/advanced_examples/pairwise_gp.html)
- **LILO: Bayesian Optimization with Natural Language Feedback** (Kobalczyk, Lin, Letham, Zhao, Balandat, Bakshy; ICML 2026, PMLR 306). An LLM converts free-form feedback and prior knowledge into structured preference signals, which feed a GP proxy model with calibrated uncertainty. The LLM assists rather than optimizes. It reportedly outperforms conventional preferential BO and LLM-only optimizers, especially with limited feedback (no numbers in abstract) — [arXiv 2510.17671](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17671)
- **Interactive Differential Evolution for headphone target curves** (Ravizza, Volk, Stegenborg-Andersen [FORCE SenseLab], Villegas, Pei [Univ. Aizu]; arXiv Dec 2025, not peer-reviewed). Ten one-octave bands (31 Hz–16 kHz), initial gains ±3 dB, population of 5, 8 generations, F = 0.2, CR = 0.7, stimuli loudness-aligned to −18 LUFS. 24 participants; sessions of 6.5–50 min (mean 31.5). Population SD fell from ~1.7–1.85 dB to ~0.98–0.99 dB (the paper reports conflicting figures). The best evolved curve was preferred over the initial one, but only modestly (odds 1.29×, mean 3.74 vs 3.51). There was no track effect — [arXiv 2512.08313](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08313v1)
- Patents: a genetic algorithm over binary choices between two EQ settings — [US 9685927 "Genetic EQ-setup"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9685927). A cochlear-implant tuning UI framed as a **dueling bandits** problem — [US 10617334](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10617334). An "equalization preference learning" patent — [US20140272883A1](https://www.google.com.au/patents/US20140272883)
- Apple's Hearing Test uses **GP classification** for audiogram estimation. This is the same Bayesian active-learning family applied to threshold estimation — [Apple white paper](https://images.apple.com/health/pdf/Hearing_Health_Features_on_AirPods_Pro_2_October_2024.pdf)
- Newer 2026 preprints exist on "Local Preferential Bayesian Optimization" and PBO with crash feedback (not reviewed here) — [arXiv 2606.02351](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.02351); [arXiv 2604.01776](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.01776)

### Inferences
- A practical design is **PairwiseGP + EUBO over a 2–4-D offset space** (bass shelf gain, treble shelf/tilt, optional 3 kHz presence, optional low-shelf corner), anchored at "measured headphone → target". Expect convergence in ~10–20 A/B trials. Use loudness-matched stimuli (the IDE paper used −18 LUFS alignment) so louder doesn't win.
- Cold start: initialize the GP mean from population segments (Olive) and optional questionnaire priors (age, "I like more bass"). Hierarchical/multi-task GP across users is possible only if users opt in to sharing.
- Passive usage data (manual EQ tweaks, skips, volume changes) can be treated as weak, noisy implicit comparisons ("user moved bass +2 dB" implies the new state is preferred over the old state in this context). This is an inference. I found no published validation of implicit-feedback EQ learning.
- Per-context learning: the simplest sound option is **separate or shared-kernel GPs keyed by context (headphone, content type, maybe level)**, i.e. a product kernel over (EQ params × context). This is functionally a contextual dueling-bandit setup. Treat genre as a context only if the data show a difference for that user.
- LILO-style hybrid (LLM turns "a bit less sibilant" into structured preference constraints, and BO stays the optimizer) is the most principled way to merge the NL feature with the learning feature. It is recent (2026) and unproven for audio.

### Gaps
- No published **contextual bandit** applied to per-genre/per-context consumer EQ was found.
- No open-source repo implementing pairwise-GP **headphone EQ** end-to-end was found. BoTorch/BoFire are general-purpose.
- I found no study on how many A/B trials a *trained audiophile* needs vs naive listeners, or on test-retest stability of BO-derived EQ.

## 4. Semantic / natural-language EQ (SocialEQ, SAFE, Audealize, Text2FX, LLM2Fx, LLM tool-calling) and guardrails

### Takeaway
Two decades of semantic-audio work (SocialEQ/Audealize, SAFE) show that some descriptors map consistently to spectral changes ("tinny", "bright", "warm") but many are noisy, many-to-many and listener-dependent. Recent work splits into (a) **CLAP-embedding optimization** (Text2FX, ICASSP 2025; FxSearcher), which is audio-aware but slow and inconsistent on abstract words, and (b) **LLM parameter prediction** (LLM2Fx, WASPAA 2025; LLM2Fx-Tools, ICLR 2026; B&O population-aligned EQ, 2026; InstructFX2FX, DAFx 2026). In (b), even 3–8B open models output JSON EQ parameters competitive with GPT-4o. The 2026 consensus direction is **"LLM plans / CLAP or user-feedback refines"** and **"output a distribution, not one curve"**. No study evaluates LLM-generated EQ for *headphone listening* with audiophile-grade listening tests.

### Cited Findings
**Crowdsourced descriptor datasets (older, 2013–2017)**
- **SocialEQ** (Cartwright & Pardo, ISMIR 2013): a web app learns an EQ curve for a user-supplied descriptor by having the user rate 40 EQ-modified versions of a sound (15 repeats for consistency). It produces relative boost/cut over **40 bands**, and had learned **324 distinct words in 731 sessions** — [ISMIR 2013 paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000173.pdf); [word-embeddings paper describing dataset](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.08898)
- Follow-up: **887 participants, 2,322 sessions** via Mechanical Turk, with descriptors ranked by agreement. **"tinny" had the top agreement score; "warm" was the most frequent word (64 sessions)** — [Cartwright & Pardo, CI 2014](https://interactiveaudiolab.github.io/assets/papers/cartwright_pardo_collintel_2014.pdf)
- SocialEQ data as used downstream: **1,595 samples (918 English), 388 unique English descriptors**, each with 40 EQ values and a consistency rating — [arXiv 2202.08898](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.08898)
- **Audealize** (Seetharaman & Pardo, JAES): descriptive-term interface for EQ and reverb built on the crowdsourced vocabulary of hundreds of terms. Users can teach new terms — [Audealize JAES PDF](https://pseeth.github.io/public/papers/seetharaman_pardo_audealize_jaes.pdf)
- **SAFE** (Stables et al., ISMIR 2014) is a plugin-embedded system to collect semantic descriptors with parameters and audio features. The SAFE EQ data contain **582 "warm" and 531 "bright" entries**, with "high within-term variance … suggesting disagreement amongst participants". The EQ is 2 shelves plus 3 peaking biquads, and warm/bright were mapped onto a 2-D control — [SAFE 2014 paper](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/bitstream/handle/123456789/12589/De%20Man%20SAFE%20A%20SYSTEM%20FOR%202014%20Published.pdf?sequence=2); [Semantically Controlled Adaptive Equalisation](https://www.open-access.bcu.ac.uk/3258/1/Semantically%20Controlled%20Adaptive%20Equalisation.pdf)
- A 2026 paper notes SAFE provides a retrieval framework from plugin metadata, but not a curated dataset suitable for training generative models — [arXiv 2603.16682](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.16682v1)
- A prior study cited in Text2FX: listeners associate "bright" with boosting >>2 kHz and cutting <<2 kHz — [Text2FX arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.18847v2)

**CLAP-based optimization**
- **Text2FX** (Chu, O'Reilly, Barnett, Pardo; ICASSP 2025): single-instance gradient optimization of DDSP effect parameters (6-band PEQ, 18 params; dasp-pytorch) so the CLAP audio embedding moves toward the text embedding. No model retraining. It uses Adam, lr 1e-2, 600 iterations and 3 restarts. Listener study: 167 valid participants via Prolific, 924 evaluations. Percent positive for EQ: cosine 48.3%, directional 45.5%, random 22.2%. It works best on concrete/imagery prompts and is inconsistent on abstract prompts (the authors attribute this to CLAP's AudioSet training) — [arXiv 2409.18847](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.18847v2); [project page](https://anniejchu.github.io/text2fx)
- The Text2EQ prototype UI adds refinement on top of Text2FX for a 6-band EQ — [Text2EQ poster](https://anniejchu.github.io/research/text2eq/468_poster.pdf)
- **FxSearcher**: a gradient-free alternative that searches effect parameters against a CLAP objective — [arXiv 2511.14138](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2511.14138)

**LLM parameter prediction**
- **LLM2Fx** (Doh, Koo, Martínez-Ramírez, Liao, Nam, Mitsufuji; Sony/KAIST; **WASPAA 2025**). Zero-shot text → JSON parameters for a 6-band PEQ (18 params) and reverb. The system prompt casts the model as "an expert audio engineer and music producer" and demands "ONLY a valid JSON object", optionally with DSP features, DSP code and 5 few-shot examples. EQ MMD (lower is better; FXenc embeddings): random 0.53, **Text2FX-style optimization 0.55**, **GPT-4o 0.22**, Llama-3.2-1B 0.64, **Llama-3.2-3B 0.27**, Llama-3.1-8B 0.25, Llama-3.3-70B 0.24, Mistral-7B 0.30 → 0.22 with all context; ground-truth bound 0.09. The vocabulary was only 7 EQ words (warm, soft, harsh, calm, loud, bright, heavy). Stated limitations: single effect, single-track instruments, one sample per instrument, subjective many-to-many labels — [arXiv 2505.20770v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20770v2); [GitHub SonyResearch/LLM2Fx](https://github.com/SonyResearch/LLM2Fx)
- **LLM2Fx-Tools** (Sony AI/KAIST; **ICLR 2026**): a multimodal tool-calling LLM that, with chain-of-thought planning, selects effect types, orders the chain and estimates parameters. It introduces the **LP-Fx dataset, 101K conversational examples with tool calls and CoT** — [arXiv 2512.01559](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.01559v1); [ICLR 2026 poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10009732)
- **Population-Aligned Audio Reproduction With LLM-Based Equalizers** (Stylianou, Francombe, Martínez-Nuevo, Shepstone [Bang & Olufsen], Tan [Aalborg]; arXiv Jan 2026, under review at IEEE JSTSP). It treats prompt→EQ as **one-to-many** and predicts a *distribution* matching how a population would set it. Dataset: 120 prompts × 11 listeners (1,320 annotations), using B&O's 2-D "Beosonic" control (smile curve × bass/treble tilt, ±6 dB). Models: **Phi-3.5-mini (3.8B, open)** vs GPT-4o-mini, with ICL, RAG, LoRA and prefix tuning. All beat random and static presets; Phi-3.5-mini was not significantly different from GPT-4o-mini, and LoRA was best but not significantly better than ICL. Specific prompts gave consistent settings, abstract prompts gave widely dispersed ones (generalized variance mean 41.6, range 2.19–250.6). Stated limitations: small data, no audio conditioning, no perceptual validation, personalization left to future work — [arXiv 2601.09448](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09448v1)
- **InstructFX2FX** (DAFx 2026; arXiv Jun 2026): **multi-turn** NL refinement. The LLM plans the chain and initial parameters, and CLAP-guided optimization refines across turns to avoid "parameter drift" from re-prompting. CLAP refinement lowered target-directed DSP-feature MMD on **9 of 10** descriptor transitions vs LLM-only re-prompting. Unstable for non-differentiable effects. Code available — [arXiv 2606.22005](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22005); [DAFx26 paper](https://www.dafx.de/paper-archive/2026/papers/DAFx26_demo_74.pdf); [GitHub vaclisinc/InstructFX2FX](https://github.com/vaclisinc/InstructFX2FX)

**Structured output / tool-calling mechanics (for guardrails)**
- Apple Foundation Models **guided generation** is constrained decoding into a `@Generable` Swift type. The framework "guarantees" that the output is an instance of that type, and tool calling is supported — [WWDC25 session 286](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/286/); [Foundation Models docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/foundationmodels)
- Ollama structured outputs accept a JSON Schema in `format` (or OpenAI-compatible `response_format`) and constrain generation to the schema. Caveats: slower decoding, complex schemas may need bigger models, and some models do not reliably honor tool/response schemas, so validate client-side and retry — [Ollama docs](https://docs.ollama.com/capabilities/structured-outputs); [Arize community thread](https://community.arize.com/x/phoenix-support/msg_5naXhy8fq8Dd/troubleshooting-ollama-issues-with-structured-outp)
- AutoEq's parametric output format is a list of filters (type, Fc, Q, gain) that the user then loads into an EQ app — [AutoEq (fork mirror)](https://github.com/ajawu/AutoEq)

### Inferences
- The defensible product pattern combines several of these results: the LLM interprets the request into a **small, typed "delta" over the current EQ** (e.g., `{band: "presence 2–5 kHz", direction: "cut", magnitude_db: 1.5, q: 1.0}`) using a vocabulary grounded in SocialEQ/SAFE-style descriptor→region tables. Deterministic code then clamps and applies it. The LLM should not emit a whole curve. This mirrors the LLM2Fx JSON pattern and the LILO "LLM-as-feedback-translator" idea.
- Guardrails derived from the evidence and domain practice (inferences, not tested in sources): (1) schema-constrained output (`@Generable` / JSON Schema); (2) hard clamps, e.g. per-filter gain ±6 dB (B&O used ±6 dB), cumulative boost cap, Q range ~0.3–6, frequency 20 Hz–20 kHz, max N filters; (3) automatic preamp = −(max positive gain of summed response) to prevent clipping; (4) loudness-matched A/B of before/after so the user judges tone, not level; (5) "explain what I changed" text plus one-tap undo.
- Because abstract words map inconsistently (SAFE variance, B&O dispersion, Text2FX polarization), the app should **ask a clarifying A/B** when a word is ambiguous ("warm" → a 2-candidate A/B: more 150–300 Hz vs less 3–8 kHz) rather than commit. This bridges NL and preference learning.
- LLM2Fx and the B&O paper both show small (~3–4B) models are near-GPT-4o on this narrow mapping task, which makes on-device feasible (see Q7).

### Gaps
- No study evaluates LLM-generated EQ for **headphone music listening by trained/audiophile listeners** with a double-blind test. Existing evaluations use embedding distances (MMD) or crowd ratings of single-instrument clips.
- I did not retrieve an authoritative, quantitative descriptor→frequency table (e.g., the "warm" band boundaries from SocialEQ/SAFE). Only "bright" (>>2 kHz) was confirmed in sources here.
- The SAFE dataset's current availability and licence, and the B&O dataset release, were not confirmed.
- "CLAP-based approaches" beyond Text2FX/FxSearcher/InstructFX2FX (e.g., other 2026 DAFx/ICASSP papers) were not exhaustively surveyed.

## 5. Genre/mood/context detection for automatic EQ switching

### Takeaway
On-device music tagging is mature and practical. Options include Essentia's TensorFlow models (Discogs-EffNet with 400 styles, MusiCNN with 50 tags, real-time demos), Apple's built-in SoundAnalysis classifier (~300 sound classes, instrument families, not genres) and custom Core ML via Create ML. But the preference science (Q1) gives little reason to switch EQ by **genre**. Switching by **headphone, content type (stereo vs spatial) and possibly level** is better supported. Player metadata (genre tags) is the cheapest and most private signal.

### Cited Findings
- Essentia `discogs-effnet-bs64` is trained multi-label on **400 Discogs styles**, with 16 kHz input. Essentia offers classification heads (genre, mood, instrument, etc.) on these embeddings — [Essentia models](https://essentia.upf.edu/models.html); [TensorflowPredictEffnetDiscogs](https://essentia.upf.edu/reference/std_TensorflowPredictEffnetDiscogs.html)
- Essentia has a real-time auto-tagging tutorial using **MusiCNN** on system audio loopback, with patch size 64 (~1 s) to increase prediction rate. Real-time Discogs-style prediction is shown in Essentia.js browser demos. Essentia TF builds exist for Linux/macOS, and the docs say real-time processing works cross-platform including iOS — [real-time tutorial](https://essentia.upf.edu/tutorial_tensorflow_real-time_auto-tagging.html); [demos](https://essentia.upf.edu/demos.html); [TensorFlow Audio Models in Essentia, ICASSP 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07393)
- Real-time capability depends on model cost and receptive field. No mobile/embedded Discogs-EffNet benchmarks were found — [Essentia TF paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07393)
- The open-source `essentia-autotagger` writes genre/mood tags to a library in batch using Discogs-EffNet — [PyPI essentia-autotagger](https://pypi.org/project/essentia-autotagger/)
- Apple SoundAnalysis (WWDC21) has a built-in classifier for **300+ sound types**, including instrument families and vocalizations, but genre labels were not confirmed. Custom classifiers can be trained with Create ML `MLSoundClassifier` and run via `SNClassifySoundRequest` — [WWDC21 10036](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10036); [MLSoundClassifier docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/createml/mlsoundclassifier)
- Content type matters for preferred headphone response (Harman target for stereo, individualized flat for binaural) — [Engel et al. 2020](https://research.facebook.com/file/283503763627038/Listener-Preferred-Headphone-Frequency-Response-for-Stereo-and-Spatial-Audio-Content.pdf)
- B&O frames EQ as needing change with "mood, location, or social setting", the motivation for their NL approach — [arXiv 2601.09448](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09448v1)
- Privacy pattern reference: Apple processes ear-scan camera data entirely on-device and syncs the profile with E2E encryption — [Apple privacy: Personalized Spatial Audio](https://www.apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/personalized-spatial-audio/)

### Inferences
- Priority of context keys for automatic switching: (1) output device/headphone model (deterministic, highest value); (2) content type (spatial/binaural vs stereo); (3) playback level band; (4) genre/mood only if a user's own data show divergent preferences per genre (learned, not assumed).
- Prefer player-supplied genre metadata (Now Playing / library tags) over audio analysis. If audio tagging is used, run it on-device (Essentia/Core ML) on short windows with hysteresis, never upload audio, and make it opt-in. Usage logs (track, genre, time, EQ changes) are sensitive behavioral data and should stay local by default.
- Audio-classifier latency and flapping (misclassifying mid-track) are UX risks. Switch at track boundaries with crossfaded EQ transitions.

### Gaps
- **PANNs** and other tagging families were not researched here, and no Core ML conversion benchmarks of Essentia/MusiCNN/PANNs on Apple silicon were found.
- The exact list of Apple's built-in SoundAnalysis classes (whether any music-genre classes exist) was not confirmed.
- No study was found showing that automatic per-genre EQ switching improves satisfaction vs a single personalized EQ.

## 6. Hearing tests on a Mac with headphones: feasibility, calibration, open source, disclaimers

### Takeaway
Self-administered pure-tone tests can approach clinical accuracy **only with known, calibrated transducers** and quiet conditions, as Apple shows with its own AirPods and FDA-reviewed validation. On a Mac with arbitrary headphones, absolute thresholds (dB HL) are not trustworthy without a per-model reference SPL, and calibration does not transfer even between units of the same model. Relative, per-ear, per-frequency *differences* and "how much quieter can you still hear X" are more robust. Open-source audiometry exists (Android/.NET/hardware). Consumer products frame results as non-medical, and Mimi moved from CE class-1 medical to the ANSI/CTA-2118 consumer-wellness standard.

### Cited Findings
- Earlier validated apps differed from standardized automated audiometry by **>8 dB HL in a booth and >14 dB HL in a waiting room**. Different headphones cause large test-signal amplitude variation, and **calibration cannot be transferred to another device even of the same model** — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00744/full)
- With calibrated audiometric headphones in a booth (68 normal-hearing adults), an app gave thresholds highly similar to standardized automated audiometry — [Frontiers 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00744/full)
- An iPad app with calibrated Sennheiser HD 300 (30 adults) agreed closely with conventional audiometry except at 6 and 8 kHz, where adjusted calibration values were recommended — [Univ. of Manchester](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/validity-and-reliability-of-an-automatic-audiometry-application-f/)
- Chen et al. (AJA 2024): headphones were more accurate than earphones, booth testing was more accurate, and accuracy was lower for people over 70 and under 18 — [ASHA AJA 2024](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/full/10.1044/2024_AJA-24-00002)
- DuoTone (AJA 2022) studied test–retest reliability of unsupervised smartphone hearing tests in uncontrolled environments — [ASHA AJA 2022](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/2022_AJA-21-00191?download=true)
- Mobile screening audiometer accuracy in normal-hearing adults — [PMC7201107](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7201107/)
- Standards: **ISO 389-9** is the preferred protocol for headphone reference levels. ANSI (2018) requires stating calibration methods and justifying how RETSPLs were derived — [PMC9308501](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9308501). Many closed-source apps do not fully conform to ANSI S3.6 — [JOSS: Audiometry framework](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.02016.pdf)
- Apple's Hearing Test (AirPods Pro only): GP-classification audiogram estimation. FDA-supported validation (n = 202 supplemental; ±5 dB for >98% of gain differences). Adults 18+ only — [Apple white paper](https://images.apple.com/health/pdf/Hearing_Health_Features_on_AirPods_Pro_2_October_2024.pdf); [FDA DEN230081 mirror](https://fda.innolitics.com/device/DEN230081)
- Open-source audiometry implementations:
  - **Audiometry**: C#/WPF framework for pure-tone, speech and other tests — [SourceForge](https://sourceforge.net/projects/audiometry-hearing-loss/); [JOSS](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.02016.pdf)
  - **woheller69/audiometry** (Android, F-Droid): calibration either with 18–35-year-old normal-hearing listeners (biological calibration) or a simpler 1 kHz-only calibration — [F-Droid](https://f-droid.org/en/packages/org.woheller69.audiometry)
  - **VibrasticLab/pikoakustik**: archived 2022, with calibrated SPL scaling and per-headphone tone models — [GitHub](https://github.com/VibrasticLab/pikoakustik)
  - **bzeiss/hearcal** (purpose not verified) — [GitHub](https://github.com/bzeiss/hearcal)
  - Masalski's Android Hearing Test app has a peer-reviewed calibration method — [Dovepress MDER 2024](https://dovepress.com/the-hearing-test-app-for-android-devices-distinctive-features-of-pure--peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-MDER)
- Disclaimers in industry: Jabra says its test isn't a substitute for a hearing professional or a medical diagnosis — [Jabra](https://www.jabra.com/en-au/hearing/hearing-test). Mimi: not medical advice, and no longer a CE medical device; it follows ANSI/CTA-2118 — [Mimi FAQ](https://mimi.io/faq/products-and-solutions). Samsung frames Adapt Sound as an accessibility tool — [SamMobile](https://www.sammobile.com/news/one-ui-tip-get-the-best-audio-quality-adapt-sound/)
- Mimi claims its MT test also helps characterize headphones so results stay reliable across hardware (vendor claim, no independent validation found) — [Mimi MT blog](https://mimi.io/blog/how-to-interpret-your-hearing-test-results-mt-test-for-sound-personalization)

### Inferences
- For an open-source Mac app: (1) Only offer threshold testing for headphone models with a known measured sensitivity/FR (e.g., from a measurement database), and label it "relative hearing profile", not dB HL. (2) Use relative left/right and across-frequency comparisons, which are robust to unknown absolute level, plus a "biological calibration" option at 1 kHz (as woheller69 does). (3) Show disclaimers and refer to an audiologist above simple asymmetry/notch thresholds (e.g., noise notch near 4 kHz). (4) Do not market it as a hearing-loss diagnosis, which would raise medical-device regulatory issues.
- For audiophiles, the hearing test is better used to **bound** the EQ (avoid pushing gain into regions they can't hear, and flag asymmetry) than to apply prescription-style gain. Prescription gain (NAL-type) is designed for speech audibility in hearing loss, not for music preference.
- A GP-classification audiogram (Apple's approach) is the modern efficient method. It can be implemented with the same GP tooling as preference learning.

### Gaps
- The ANSI/CTA-2118 content (requirements for consumer hearing tests) was not retrieved.
- No open-source **macOS** audiometry app was found. Mac output-chain issues (system volume, Bluetooth codec gain, AirPods adaptive processing) were not investigated, and they belong partly to the other researchers' scope.
- Regulatory specifics (FDA "general wellness" vs device, EU MDR) for an open-source hearing test were not researched.

## 7. On-device ML on Apple silicon vs cloud LLM APIs for an open-source app

### Takeaway
The text→EQ-delta task is narrow, and evidence shows 3–4B models perform close to GPT-4o on it (LLM2Fx: Llama-3.2-3B 0.27 vs GPT-4o 0.22 MMD; B&O: Phi-3.5-mini ≈ GPT-4o-mini). Apple's on-device Foundation Models framework (macOS 26+, ~3B model, guided generation into typed Swift structs, tool calling, **4,096-token context**) is therefore a practical, free, private default on Apple-Intelligence Macs. Ollama/LM Studio (JSON-schema constrained output) and bring-your-own-key cloud APIs are optional fallbacks. Preference learning (GP/BO) and tagging (Essentia/Core ML) are cheap enough to run on-device.

### Cited Findings
- Apple Intelligence Foundation Language Models Tech Report 2025: a **~3B-parameter on-device model** using KV-cache sharing and **2-bit quantization-aware training**, plus a server MoE model for Private Cloud Compute — [Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/apple-foundation-models-tech-report-2025); [arXiv 2507.13575](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.13575)
- The Foundation Models framework requires macOS 26+ on Apple silicon with Apple Intelligence enabled. Guided generation is constrained decoding into `@Generable` types, and tool calling is supported — [Foundation Models docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/foundationmodels); [WWDC25 session 286](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/286/); [Rust crate README noting requirements](https://docs.rs/crate/foundation-models/0.9.0/source/README.md)
- The context window is **4,096 tokens per session** (input + output). Overflow throws `.exceededContextWindowSize`, and there is **no built-in fallback to cloud providers**. Apple says the limit may change across versions or devices, and there is no token-estimation API — [TN3193](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technotes/tn3193-managing-the-on-device-foundation-model-s-context-window); [Apple Developer Forums 833642](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/833642)
- An anecdotal forum report describes degraded guided generation/tool calling and more refusals after OS 26.4 (single report) — [Apple Developer Forums, Foundation Models topic](https://developer.apple.com/forums/topics/machine-learning-and-ai/machine-learning-and-ai-foundation-models)
- Ollama: JSON-Schema structured outputs via `format`, an OpenAI-compatible `response_format`, and native tool calling. Caveats: slower, complex schemas need larger models, and schema adherence varies by model — [Ollama docs](https://docs.ollama.com/capabilities/structured-outputs); [Arize community](https://community.arize.com/x/phoenix-support/msg_5naXhy8fq8Dd/troubleshooting-ollama-issues-with-structured-outp)
- Small-model evidence for this task: LLM2Fx EQ MMD for Llama-3.2-1B 0.64 (worse than random 0.53), **3B 0.27**, 8B 0.25, 70B 0.24, GPT-4o 0.22 — [LLM2Fx](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20770v2). B&O: Phi-3.5-mini (3.8B) vs GPT-4o-mini was not significantly different, and LoRA fine-tuning was trained on a single RTX 3090 — [arXiv 2601.09448](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09448v1)
- Apple processes sensitive personalization (ear scans) on-device — [Apple privacy](https://www.apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/personalized-spatial-audio/)

### Inferences
- Recommended tiering: (1) Apple Foundation Models with a `@Generable` EQ-delta struct, a compact descriptor glossary in the instructions (fits easily in 4K tokens) and tool calls such as `getCurrentEQ()`, `proposeDelta()` and `previewAB()`. (2) Local Ollama/LM Studio for Macs without Apple Intelligence or users who want bigger models. (3) BYOK cloud (OpenAI/Anthropic/Gemini) as opt-in, sending only text and the current parameter set, never audio or listening history.
- Avoid 1B-class models for this mapping. LLM2Fx shows they fall below random.
- A **LoRA adapter** trained on SocialEQ/SAFE-derived pairs is a feasible open-source contribution for local models. Apple's framework also supports custom adapters, but that was not verified in this pass.
- GP preference learning (2–4 dims, <100 observations) is trivially cheap on CPU. It can be implemented in Swift directly, without Python/BoTorch, using a Laplace-approximated probit GP.

### Gaps
- **MLX / MLX-LM** performance numbers for 3–8B models on M-series Macs were not retrieved.

## 8. Proven vs speculative, and audiophile-community reception

### Takeaway
**Proven (peer-reviewed and/or regulator-reviewed):** a population target (Harman) is preferred by most listeners for stereo music. Segments exist, differing mainly in bass/treble tilt, with age and experience correlates. Pairwise-comparison Bayesian optimization finds preferred gain settings in ~10–20 trials (hearing-aid literature, Widex in production). Calibrated self-administered audiometry can reach about ±5 dB (Apple with its own hardware). **Plausible but under-validated:** LLM text→EQ (only embedding-metric and crowd evaluations; no audiophile blind tests), personalized-from-hearing-test EQ for normal-hearing music listeners (vendor studies only), CLAP optimization (inconsistent on abstract words). **Speculative / unsupported:** automatic per-genre EQ, mood/context EQ switching, passive implicit-feedback learning, "hearing-test-corrected" audiophile EQ on uncalibrated headphones. Community discussion is skeptical of black-box personalization, but it uses LLMs informally as a starting point for EQ, and debates the Harman target itself.

### Inferences
- For a demanding audiophile audience, credibility depends on: (a) starting from a **measurement-based correction to a published target** (AutoEq-style), with personalization as an explicit, visible offset; (b) **loudness-matched, blind A/B** for every learned or LLM-proposed change; (c) showing the actual filters (Fc/Q/gain), with no hidden "magic"; (d) labelling the LLM as an *interface* to EQ, not an authority. The research only supports it as a translator of words into small spectral moves.
- Marketing "AI personalized sound" will likely be met with placebo skepticism. Publishing the methodology (GP-preference learning with loudness matching, citing Olive/Nielsen) and letting users export or inspect their learned model is the strongest counter.
